# Test each drift term in simulation

A sim-trained model's chance of reaching <0.5% drift depends far more on four bias terms than on which flow network sits at the front: wave motion, heading, height and current. Each term can be set up as a controlled experiment in mare-realtime. UFM was a sensible starting point, but it is not the front end to carry the experiment forward. Its distinctive wide-baseline matching has little to do over open water, where nothing is revisited. Its covisibility mask says whether a pixel is matchable, not how far wrong the match is. Its released checkpoints are **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0**. Its smallest public model, a 0.3B-parameter ViT-L, needs 28 ms on an RTX 5090 and has no Jetson measurement. The recommended build is a retrained SEA-RAFT(M)-class flow network with per-pixel uncertainty, with NeuFlow v2 as the faster fallback. It feeds a temporal water-motion module that exploits wave dispersion, and an error-state filter that carries wind, current, barometer and heading-bias states. The literature supplies candidate methods and a bar to beat. The best published GNSS-free fixed-wing flights reach **about 2.5–5% of distance** over land, the best flown camera-class heading result is **0.51° RMSE**, and the best optical current retrieval is **0.06–0.09 m/s** with an Earth-fixed reference. Three limits are physics, not track record, and the experiments should treat them as controls rather than goals. First, a spatially uniform surface current produces the same image sequence as ego-motion, so no imagery reveals it. Second, water texture carries no north, so absolute heading needs the sun, the sky or a sensor. Third, flow alone has no metric scale; it needs dispersion physics or a ranging sensor. The first experiments should measure how much each of these costs and whether learnable cues recover any of it.

## UFM's best capabilities sit idle over open water

UFM (CMU, NeurIPS 2025) is one transformer on a DINO backbone that returns dense flow and a per-pixel covisibility mask. Its authors report it **28% more accurate than UniMatch** on flow, and **62% less error and 6.7x faster than RoMa** on wide-baseline matching ([arXiv 2506.09278](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09278)). The case for it is real. It covers both frame-to-frame motion and re-acquisition after a gap or a hard yaw, the covisibility output gates bad pixels, DINO features are the strongest generic prior for weak texture, and the team already has checkpoints and training code.

Over open water, half of that case has nothing to do. Wide-baseline matching earns its keep through re-localisation and loop closure. The same holds for the SLAM back ends of DROID-SLAM, DPV-SLAM, MASt3R-SLAM and VGGT-SLAM, which assume static structure that gets revisited ([arXiv 2408.01654](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.01654v1); [arXiv 2412.12392](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.12392)). The sea offers neither. What remains useful is dense flow plus a confidence, and many smaller models produce that. A filter also wants a variance, not a mask. SEA-RAFT is trained with a mixture-of-Laplace loss and exposes a per-pixel uncertainty scale ([GitHub SEA-RAFT](https://github.com/princeton-vl/SEA-RAFT)). MAC-VO predicts full 2D flow covariance with an NLL loss, and that covariance is the main source of its gain over DPVO and TartanVO on TartanAir v2 Hard ([arXiv 2409.09479v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09479v2)). TLIO's ablations show learned per-measurement covariance beating hand-tuned constants inside an EKF ([arXiv 2007.01867](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.01867)).

Licence and runtime close the question for anything that ships. UFM's code is BSD-3-Clause, but its checkpoints "inherit the licenses of the underlying training datasets" and "may not be used for commercial purposes (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)" ([GitHub UFM](https://github.com/UniFlowMatch/UFM)). The public checkpoints name only DINOv2-L and DINOv2-G initialisations. DINOv3's custom licence forbids use for activities subject to ITAR and for "military or warfare purposes", requires derivatives to carry the same licence, and lets Meta amend the terms with immediate effect ([DINOv3 LICENSE.md](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov3/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). DINOv2 is plain Apache-2.0 ([DINOv2 LICENSE](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov2/blob/main/LICENSE)), so it is the safe backbone for a navigation product that could be sold into defence. On speed, the authors report **28 ms (UFM-Base-DINOv2L-init, 0.3B)** to 44 ms (UFM-Refine, 0.4B) on an RTX 5090, and the real-time UFM-Tiny is announced but not released ([GitHub UFM](https://github.com/UniFlowMatch/UFM)). The nearest Jetson reference points are discouraging: ViT-encoder iterative stereo runs at **2.2–8.5 FPS** under TensorRT FP16 on Orin ([arXiv 2603.26250](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.26250)). My inference from GPU class is that UFM will not hold 10 Hz on a Jetson without distillation. UFM remains valuable as a research baseline and an offline teacher.

## Smaller RAFT-family flow wins on the Jetson and licence axes

Frame-to-frame motion is the job, so the comparison is between dense flow models on runtime, uncertainty and licence. Rigid-benchmark accuracy is only a tiebreaker, since none of these models has been evaluated on open water.

SEA-RAFT is the strongest replacement. It reported **Spring EPE 3.69**, state of the art at publication, at "at least 2.3x faster than existing methods" ([arXiv 2405.14793](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14793)), and its Laplace head gives the filter the variance UFM lacks. Its Jetson case is arithmetic, not measurement. On an Orin Nano at Sintel resolution it takes 0.524 s per frame ([arXiv 2408.10161v3](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10161v3)). At about 512×384, roughly 0.44x the pixels, that falls to about 0.23 s, so a module 2–3x faster than the Nano would reach 10 Hz. NeuFlow v2 holds the only published real-time Orin claim, at 10–70x the speed of comparably accurate methods ([arXiv 2408.10161](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10161)). WAFT leads on Spring, Sintel and KITTI but needs a depth-pretrained backbone to lead everywhere ([arXiv 2506.21526](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21526v3)). Single-pass matching on frozen DINOv2 plus a depth model reaches **Sintel Final EPE 2.81 without refinement** ([arXiv 2605.08000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08000)), which justifies ablating a small DINOv2 trunk. Two deployment constraints apply to all candidates. RAFT-style flow has lost major accuracy under INT8 ([arXiv 2208.02808](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02808)), so the correlation lookup and update block should stay in FP16. TensorRT engines also need offline, per-device builds, because the ONNX Runtime TensorRT provider has been seen to stall on Orin ([arXiv 2608.17874](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17874)). Weight licences remain unsettled for every model trained on FlyingThings, Sintel or KITTI ([GitHub NeuFlow_v2](https://github.com/neufieldrobotics/NeuFlow_v2)), so the shipped weights should come from data the team controls. Learned flow is known to work on water texture: RAFT generalised to lab and field river imagery ([Imperial Spiral](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/5ce1094f-d59f-4ea3-95b7-12efb7d79091)), and a multi-frame RAFT trained on simulated flow references handled UAV river video ([KTU ePubl](https://epubl.ktu.edu/object/elaba:281557088/index.html)).

## Four budget terms, four experiments

Drift over tens of kilometres comes from biases, not noise. A 0.29° heading bias alone produces sin(0.29°) ≈ 0.51% cross-track error, the whole budget. A working allocation is therefore needed. Splitting the budget equally in root-sum-square across three independent biases gives each about 0.29% of distance: **0.17° of heading, 0.35 m of height at 120 m, and 0.17 m/s of water-relative velocity bias at 60 m/s**. That is my arithmetic, and it sets the pass thresholds below. Every experiment measures the *mean* error per slice (sea state, sun elevation, altitude, speed), not RMS, because the mean is what integrates into drift.

| Term | Physics limit vs. learnable cue | Bar to beat from the literature | Pass threshold in sim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wave phase motion | Learnable: phase speed depends on wavelength, advection does not | 0.06–0.09 m/s RMS, hovering, 30 s, Earth-referenced | ≤0.17 m/s mean bias at 60 m/s |
| Heading | Water has no north; sun/glint is the only cue in an RGB frame | 0.51° RMSE (sky-polarization compass, flown) | ≤0.17° residual bias where the sun is usable |
| Height / scale | Flow has no scale; dispersion ties pixels to gravity | ~1 m spread at 50 m (radar/baro over sea) | ≤0.29% scale bias |
| Uniform current | **Unobservable in principle** (Galilean invariance) | None; all retrievals use an Earth-fixed reference | Control: drift should equal the injected current |
| Non-uniform current | Gradients and wave–current interaction may leave traces | None published | Measure the fraction of current changes recovered |

### Wave phase motion is the term a visual front end can actually remove

Over water, a correct match still reports motion relative to the pattern matched: a crest moving at phase speed, foam drifting, or glint sliding with geometry. In tidal-stream LSPIV, RMSE rose from **0.22 m/s in light wind to 0.44 m/s in 31 km/h wind** ([Plymouth PEARL](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/secam-research/518)). In a windy tidal channel, LSPIV and STIV failed while the dispersion method IWaVE agreed with ADCP ([EGU25-3849](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-3849.html)). The team's own KLT measurement of 0.7–1.6 m/s apparent water velocity from hovering quads matches deep-water phase speeds for the 0.3–1.6 m wavelengths visible from those heights. Uncertainty heads will not flag this, because surface motion matches cleanly.

The model has to learn to report velocity relative to the water mass, not to the pattern. The cue is dispersion. Each component appears at ω = √(gk) + k·(U − V), so phase speed varies with wavelength while advection and ego-motion move all wavelengths together. Two frames 0.1 s apart cannot separate the two, because a 1 m crest moves about 0.125 m in that time. The model needs a temporal, multi-scale view on a grid rectified for attitude and height. Hovering-drone dispersion fits reach **0.06–0.09 m/s against ADCP from about 30 s of video** ([Hereon CopterCurrents](https://ms.hereon.de/coptercurrents/index.php.en); [IEEE vTools 2025](https://events.vtools.ieee.org/m/493102); [Streßer et al. 2017](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8049342)). That is the bar, set with a fixed camera. Airborne along-track processing shows the window can follow a moving footprint ([ROCIS, AGU 2016](https://studio.m-anage.com/agu/os16/preliminaryview.cgi/Paper91778.html)).

The experiment runs with current set to zero. Randomise the JONSWAP wind speed, fetch and peak enhancement, the swell/wind-sea mix and direction, altitude (30–400 m), ground speed (15–70 m/s) and sun position. Compare four estimators of water-relative velocity: pretrained two-frame flow (UFM and SEA-RAFT off the shelf), the same network trained on ego-motion labels, and a temporal module with windows of 1, 3, 10 and 30 s. Measure mean velocity bias against the true camera velocity relative to the water. The approach is confirmed if bias falls below 0.17 m/s at 60 m/s on every slice within a window the footprint allows (at 60 m/s the footprint moves 1.8 km in 30 s). It is refuted if bias stays tied to the phase speed of the resolvable wavelengths, which would be most visible at altitude, where only longer, faster waves (about 4 m/s at λ = 10 m) remain. The window-versus-accuracy curve this produces is not in the literature.

### Heading needs an absolute cue, and the sun is the only one in frame

Water texture cannot fix absolute heading: every direction looks statistically alike, short of a wave forecast. The model therefore has to learn to estimate the bias of the attitude source, which drifts by degrees on small UAVs ([Vitiello et al., PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8196794/)) and reached **5.54° yaw RMSE** in a GNSS-free fixed-wing study ([Ye et al., Sensors 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9099684/)). The cue in an RGB frame is the sun's azimuth, seen directly or through glint. Sun direction is computable from time and coarse position. Glint position follows from reflection geometry and Cox–Munk slope statistics, spreading with wind ([Kay et al. 2009](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/1/4/697)). A CNN sun-direction estimator has already served as a heading cue for ground-vehicle VO ([arXiv 1606.07415](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.07415)). Wave direction can add relative heading stability over minutes, but it is not tied to north. The bars to beat are the flown sky-polarization compass at **0.51° RMSE** ([IEEE 10747186](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10747186)) and, on the hardware side, gyrocompassing, which needs about **0.05°/h gyro bias at 45° latitude** to reach 0.29° ([US patent 6,477,465](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6477465)).

The experiment first needs a check that the simulator's glint matches Cox–Munk statistics, since a wrong glint model would teach a wrong cue. Then inject a randomised heading bias of a few degrees, with slow drift and heading-dependent soft-iron error, into the attitude stream. Add realistic roll and pitch error, and randomise sun elevation and azimuth, cloud cover and wind. Train a sun/glint head whose output feeds the filter's heading-bias state, and measure residual heading bias after convergence by slice. It is confirmed if the residual stays at or below 0.17° wherever the sun or glint is usable. It is refuted, in an informative way, if the residual follows the tilt-coupling law δθ·tan(sun elevation). At 45° elevation a 0.2° tilt error alone uses most of 0.29° (my geometry), so the experiment would then say how good roll and pitch must be, not that the cue fails. Overcast slices give the control: with no sun, the residual should equal the injected bias. That marks where a heading sensor (a pre-aligned high-grade gyro or a polarization compass) is needed rather than an option.

### Height can come from dispersion if scale and velocity are fitted together

Flow gives angular rate, and height turns it into metres, so a height bias becomes a velocity-scale bias. A barometer tracks short-term height changes well. But 0.35 m at sea level is about 0.04 hPa, and fronts move pressure by "a few mbar per hour" ([US patent 7,428,466](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7428466)). The visual cue is gravity. Spencer and Shah recovered real-world scale from uncalibrated sea video using the dispersion relation ([IEEE TIP 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16764277/)). In pixel units the gravity term grows as the square root of wavenumber, while the Doppler term from relative motion grows linearly. A fit across many wavelengths can therefore in principle recover metres-per-pixel and water-relative velocity together. My derivation is that a single-frequency estimate shifts by about 2% for 0.1 m/s of unmodelled motion on a 50 m wave, which is why the fit must be joint. Current retrieval works precisely by measuring this departure from still-water dispersion ([arXiv 1904.11575](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.11575)). The bar is the ranging hardware. Measured over-sea radar and barometric altimetry showed about **1 m median spread at 50 m** ([Alberi et al., Sensors 2017](https://iris.unife.it/handle/11392/2374044)). Vendor long-range radar altimeters list 0.365 m precision ([Ainstein LR-D1](https://www.sundance.com/wp-content/uploads/docs/LR-D1-2024-Data-Sheet-1.pdf)) or 0.9 m/1% ([TUALCOM](https://www.unmannedsystemstechnology.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/RADAR_ALTIMETER-Datasheet.pdf)).

The experiment uses a barometer model with drifting sea-level pressure, temperature deviation and static-port error. Randomise sea state (including swell-dominated and wind-sea-dominated spectra), altitude, current and depth, and keep true height above water as the label. Measure the dispersion head's scale bias alone, and the filter's height bias with barometer plus dispersion. It is confirmed if the joint fit keeps scale bias at or below 0.29% across sea states, insensitive to the injected current. It is refuted if bias tracks current or sea state. Nonlinear (Stokes) waves, wind-drift shear and finite depth are the likely culprits; each can be switched on separately in the simulator to attribute the error. A refutation sets the specification for a radar altimeter rather than ending the approach.

### Uniform current is a control, not a target

A spatially uniform current translates the whole surface, with its waves, foam and glint pattern, at one velocity. By Galilean invariance, the image sequence is identical to one taken with the aircraft moving the opposite way over still water. No front end, and no amount of training, can tell them apart. Every published optical current retrieval breaks the ambiguity with an Earth-fixed reference: a georeferenced hover or a GPS/INS-mapped frame ([Dugan et al. 2001](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Dugan_etal_JGR2001.pdf); [CopterCurrents](https://ms.hereon.de/coptercurrents/index.php.en)). Adding airspeed does not help. Ground velocity equals air-relative velocity plus wind, and also water-relative velocity plus current, so the two sensors together observe only wind minus current.

This makes the uniform-current run the most valuable control in the programme. Inject a constant current of 0–1 m/s in a random direction, hold everything else fixed, and measure drift. The expected floor is |U| divided by ground speed, for example 0.5% at 0.3 m/s and 60 m/s. If the trained model drifts more, current is leaking into some other bias. If it drifts **less**, it has found a shortcut that will not exist at sea: a repeating sea tile, cloud shadows locked to the ground, or texture fixed to the world rather than the water. Either result is a finding about the simulator, and both should block promotion. The floor this run measures tells the team how much the operating area's currents cost, and therefore how good a current forecast prior must be.

### Non-uniform currents may leave learnable traces

Real currents vary in space and with depth. Gradients refract and steepen waves, current fronts gather foam and slicks into lines, and vertical shear makes the effective Doppler velocity depend on wavelength. The IMU adds another handle. It measures acceleration in the ground frame, and vision measures velocity relative to the water. As the aircraft crosses a current gradient, the apparent water-relative velocity changes with no matching inertial acceleration. Over short intervals, where IMU drift is small, that mismatch reveals the *change* in current along track. The absolute mean stays unobservable. This is my derivation, and no paper has tested it.

The experiment requires the simulator to advect waves and texture with a spatially varying current and to shift the dispersion relation accordingly. Randomise fronts, eddies, tidal shear and depth profiles, include IMU errors, and add current change as an auxiliary label. Measure what fraction of current variance along track the model and filter recover, and whether any absolute current information appears beyond the forecast prior. It is confirmed if gradient and change estimates shrink the effective current term from the full current to the error in its mean. A null result means the current prior has to carry the whole term.

## Training in simulation favours modular, label-rich designs

Synthetic-only training transfers better than intuition suggests. Mainstream flow models are pretrained on synthetic data, and Mayer et al. found that variety and a staged schedule matter more than photorealism ([arXiv 1801.06397](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.06397)). AutoFlow tunes renderer hyperparameters against target-domain accuracy and beat hand-designed sets for pretraining RAFT ([arXiv 2104.14544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14544)). It maps directly onto a parametric ocean renderer, with the GNSS-scored real flights as validation. MAC-VO's covariance was trained only on synthetic TartanAir and transferred to real data without fine-tuning ([arXiv 2409.09479v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.09479v2)). Synthetic sea-clutter pretraining has also improved real marine-radar wave estimates ([Front. Mar. Sci. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1363135/pdf)). The simulator's advantage is the labels: camera motion relative to the Earth and to the water, surface velocity, true heading and true height, none of which any public dataset provides.

For real-data adaptation, self-supervised photometric losses (UFlow, SMURF) assume brightness constancy ([arXiv 2006.04902](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.04902); [arXiv 2105.07014](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.07014)). Glint, sky gradients and moving waves violate that assumption. BrightFlow's brightness-change term is the nearest fix ([WACV 2023](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/WACV2023/papers/Marsal_BrightFlow_Brightness-Change-Aware_Unsupervised_Learning_of_Optical_Flow_WACV_2023_paper.pdf)). A cleaner signal is GNSS velocity on training flights, applied to the front end's velocity output through light adapter fine-tuning. That is a proposal, not an established method. Distillation is how teacher accuracy reaches a Jetson. RoMa ships a Tiny RoMa on XFeat ([GitHub RoMa](https://github.com/Parskatt/RoMa)), but no recipe distils UFM or RoMa into a real-time flow student. For a commercial model the teacher should be RoMa, or UFM retrained from its BSD code.

On filters, Kloss, Martius and Bohg found that training through a differentiable filter helps mostly by learning heteroscedastic noise models. End-to-end fine-tuning of the sensor and process models sometimes hurt, and separate pretraining followed by fine-tuning worked best ([arXiv 2012.14313](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.14313)). Tight coupling has failure modes of its own: DBA-Fusion drifted when its keyframe logic let IMU preintegration intervals grow ([arXiv 2512.02293](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.02293)). An end-to-end sequence model might find cues nobody designed. The modular design can still learn dispersion and sun cues through explicit heads, and it keeps per-sensor dropout and each experiment above separately testable.

## Recommended build and experiment order

For the front end, retrain a SEA-RAFT(M)-class network with its Laplace uncertainty head from the BSD-3 code. Feed it the undistorted 720×1280 frame downsampled to about 512 px on the long side, at 4 iterations in FP16 with an offline-built TensorRT engine. Keep NeuFlow v2 (Apache-2.0) as the fallback, with an NLL-trained uncertainty head added, and ablate a small DINOv2 (Apache-2.0) trunk. Exclude DINOv3 on licence grounds. Keep UFM as the baseline and teacher, retraining it from BSD code if its outputs will shape shipped weights. The front end should also emit a water/land/glint mask and a health value.

Around the front end, build four further components. A geometry layer maps ego-flow on the near-planar sea to water-relative horizontal velocity using attitude, height and intrinsics, and propagates covariance. A temporal water-motion module, trained on mare-realtime labels, removes wave phase speed and jointly estimates image scale. A sun/glint head observes heading bias. An error-state EKF propagates the IMU and carries gyro and accelerometer biases, wind, current (with a forecast prior), barometric offset and scale, airspeed scale, flow-scale bias and heading bias. Only its noise models are fine-tuned end-to-end on displacement losses from 1 s to minutes, and calibration is checked with NEES/NIS on real flights.

The experiments, ranked by how much they settle per unit of effort:

| Rank | Experiment | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pretrained two-frame flow vs. true water-relative velocity on rendered sea, zero current | Size of the wave-phase bias the front end must remove; checks the simulator reproduces the 0.7–1.6 m/s hover result |
| 2 | Uniform-current control and shortcut audit | The current drift floor; whether the model is exploiting tiling, shadows or world-fixed texture |
| 3 | SEA-RAFT(M) and NeuFlow v2 latency at ~512 px on the target Jetson (not sim; runs in parallel) | Which front end fits 10 Hz |
| 4 | Temporal window (1–30 s) vs. phase-rejection bias by altitude and speed | Whether wave motion fits 0.17 m/s, and the window the architecture needs |
| 5 | Sun/glint heading-bias observer with injected AHRS bias, after a Cox–Munk glint check | Whether in-frame heading reaches 0.17°, and the attitude accuracy it requires |
| 6 | Joint dispersion scale + velocity fit with baro model and current | Whether visual scale reaches 0.29% or a radar altimeter is required |
| 7 | Non-uniform currents with IMU-vs-vision change detection | How much of the current term is recoverable beyond the prior |
| 8 | Full sensor-error stack on ≥50 km runs, differentiable EKF fine-tuning | Whether the terms close together at <0.5% in sim, before the real bench decides |

## Conclusion

Seen as an experiment, the choice of front end is a licence and runtime decision. The drift outcome depends on three other things: whether the labels teach water-relative ego-motion rather than pattern motion, whether a temporal window separates wave phase from advection, and whether the filter carries each bias as a state. A small SEA-RAFT trained on the right labels should beat a UFM trained on the wrong ones, and the first ranked experiment will show by how much. The simulator's unique value is that it can switch each bias on alone, which no flight campaign can do.

The controls matter as much as the successes. The uniform-current run and the overcast heading run should each fail to beat their physics floors. If either beats its floor, the simulator has a leak, and a sim result below 0.5% would mean nothing until it is fixed. If both behave as physics predicts and the learnable terms pass their thresholds, the residual budget becomes a precise specification: how good the current prior, the roll and pitch, and any added heading or height sensor must be. That turns "can sim-trained models reach 0.5%?" into a question the real bench can answer flight by flight.
